CASE STUDY

CarePath

A calmer way to move through a public hospital visit.

A navigation layer for public services. It starts with what a person is trying to do — see a doctor, get a certificate, chase a pension — and turns that into one clear step at a time, right up to the official government system, then hands over.

THE PROBLEM

The service usually exists.
Finding it doesn't feel like it does.

Most public-service frustration isn't the transaction itself — it's everything around it: which office handles this, what to bring, whether you're on the right step, and what happens after you submit.

  • People know the outcome they want, rarely the government service name for it.
  • Official portals ask for department-level decisions before intent is even clear.
  • Once you're in a process, there is no reliable way to tell where you stand.
  • Low connectivity, older phones and partial translations quietly exclude people.
How might we build a simple, trustworthy layer that moves people through a public-service journey step by step — without pretending to replace the government system underneath it?
APPROACH

What I was actually designing

1

Start with the outcome

Users pick what they're trying to do, not a department. “I need to see a doctor,” not “OPD registration.”

2

One step, always visible

Every journey has exactly one current step, a progress position, and one obvious next action.

3

Stay alongside the system

CarePath explains and prepares. The official service remains the source of truth for the actual transaction.

4

Design for the phone that's actually in someone's hand

Accessibility controls, read-aloud, language switching and a low-connectivity path are load-bearing, not afterthoughts.

5

No control lies about being interactive

If it looks tappable, it does something — advances a step, opens the destination, or clearly says why it is disabled.

PROCESS — 10 DAYS

Frame, then build outward

This was a solo sprint, built end to end in ten days. I front-loaded the pattern that everything else would reuse, then spent the back half making sure nothing in the interface was decorative.

Day 1–2

Frame the service problem

Defined CarePath as guidance, not a replacement portal. Started every flow from outcomes, not department structure.

Day 3–4

Build the reusable journey pattern

Service selection → step → official handoff → reference. Every later journey is this pattern, restated.

Day 5–7

Expand into four services

Healthcare, pension, certificates and grievances share the same underlying journey skeleton with different content.

Day 8

Design for access

Accessibility panel, language switching, read-aloud and a keypad-friendly concept for people without a smartphone.

Day 9

Make every interaction real

Went screen by screen removing dead-end buttons and cards that looked interactive but weren't.

Day 10

Test the whole loop

Ran all four journeys end to end, switched languages both directions, checked back/exit/restart and verified official links.

HEALTHCARE WALKTHROUGH

Healthcare, the deepest journey

Healthcare is the fully working part of the prototype — booking guidance, a live visit tracker, and the staff side that drives it.

01 — STARTING FROM INTENTWhat do you need today?

Plain-language entry points instead of department names, with a clear reminder that CarePath is the route to the service — not the hospital itself.

02 — GUIDED JOURNEYOne clear next step.

The interface keeps the current state visible while reducing the amount of information the user needs to process at once.

03 — LIVE VISITKnow where you are.

A visit tracker gives patients a clearer sense of progress while the staff side drives the journey state.

04 — OFFICIAL HANDOFFPrepare, then hand over.

CarePath explains and prepares before handing the user to the official service, keeping the boundary between guidance and transaction clear.

DESIGN DECISIONS

Accessibility is part of the architecture.

The prototype treats access as a core product requirement rather than a final checklist: readable type, short interactions, language switching, read-aloud support and a low-connectivity path are built into the journey model.

The goal wasn't to make a government portal look nicer. It was to make the journey understandable before asking someone to navigate the system underneath it.
REFLECTION

Designing the layer between intent and bureaucracy.

CarePath taught me to treat interaction architecture as the product, not simply the container around a set of screens. By defining the journey pattern first, the visual system and the different service flows could grow without losing the core promise: one clear next step.

The result is intentionally a synthetic prototype. The value of the project is in the service model, interaction decisions and working journey — not in pretending to replace a real government or hospital system.
